## 1. CONSENSUS MAP

Where all (or 3 of 4) plans independently agree. These are the highest-confidence recommendations, but confidence should be weighted by *why* they agree: some consensus flows from a shared practitioner canon (Wasserman/Hellmann, Carta, YC, Cooley — correlated sources), while other consensus was reached through genuinely independent lines (primary Massachusetts statutes and SJC cases in B and C; Plan D cites nothing yet lands on the same legal conclusions, which is convergence through independent derivation).

### Unanimous (4/4)

1. **Manager-managed LLC with a small board including one independent seat; the CEO sits ex officio.** A, C, D specify 3 seats; B specifies 5 (see Divergence #1). *Basis: shared practitioner canon (Carta, Rho, NVCA, Feld) — correlated sources, but the norm itself is well documented.*
2. **Exactly one CEO, selected by defined process, removable by the board at any time under mechanics agreed now — and removal from office never touches earned equity or member status.** All four cite or restate the ~50%-of-founder-CEOs-replaced-by-year-3 base rate (Wasserman HBR 2008) or its logic. *Mixed basis: shared sources for the statistic; independent convergence on the office/property separation.*
3. **All founder equity is earned over time ("stop the earn"), and earned/vested equity is never confiscated from a good leaver.** B, C, D explicitly ground this in Massachusetts freeze-out law (Donahue → Wilkes → Pointer → Allison — all real cases, cites verified); A reaches the same design through leaver-provision practice. *This is the strongest consensus in the set: two teams verified it against primary SJC authority independently, a third derived it uncited, and the fourth reached it from a different (practitioner) direction.*
4. **Inactive founders: objective activity definition → accrual stops automatically → keep what's earned (with votes) → lose roles, board eligibility, and future earning. No discretionary, litigable decision required.** All four converge on "self-executing economics instead of confrontation."
5. **Founder titles are role assignments, not property**; written role charters/DRI maps with quarterly objectives, annual review of CEO and founders.
6. **Retroactive IP assignment + confidentiality from every founder and worker, signed with the restatement.** (A adds the Snap/Reggie Brown case as motivation — the $157.5M settlement figure checks out.)
7. **Pre-consented Delaware C-corp conversion at (or before) the first qualified financing, plus a drag-along** — eliminating 15-holder holdout risk. All four independently name this the top investability feature.
8. **The professor's oral promise is replaced by a written ~1.0% advisory agreement, ~24-month monthly vesting, conditioned on defined engagement, with university-policy representations.** Four independent teams landed on the *same number* (1.0%) from benchmark bands (FAST expert tier ≈1.0% at earliest stage — verified; Carta advisor medians 0.21%/0.12% pre-seed/seed — verified). This is about as strong as convergence gets in this set.
9. **A dynamic/contribution ledger, if used, must hard-freeze at the first financing** (A, B, C build ledgers and freeze them; D rejects the ledger *because* it must be frozen and explained — i.e., even the dissenter agrees with the premise).
10. **Member supermajority (66⅔–75%) reserved-matters list mirroring NVCA protective provisions; short unanimity list or none.**
11. **Deadlock: escalation ladder (negotiation → mediation → arbitration/expert determination), with an appraised-value buyout as the terminal step.**

### Near-unanimous (3/4)

12. **The Massachusetts Wage Act problem (~10 unpaid employees, plus unpaid founders) is an accruing, non-waivable, treble-damages, personally-liable emergency that equity cannot settle and that precedes the equity design.** B, C, D treat it as the top-priority workstream; B and C independently verified it against primary authority (c. 149 §§148/150, c. 151 §1, *Reuter* — verified, *Segal* — verified, *Cook*, *Stanton*). **Plan A flags it only as a late-added counsel note** (merit formula §1b; open decision point 10) and does not sequence it first — the most consequential difference in emphasis in the whole set. The 3-plan position is clearly right: this is existential and time-sensitive.
13. **No shotgun/Texas-shootout clause** (B, C, D), because unpaid, cash-poor founders are systematically exploitable in shotgun mechanisms. B cites the strongest evidence (Brooks/Landeo/Spier, RAND J. Econ. 2010 — theory + experiments; verified real, see audit). **Plan A retains a sealed-bid Texas shootout as a last resort** (with mitigations). See Divergence #6.
14. **Issue the retroactive equity now, while FMV is provably nominal, with a contemporaneous valuation and protective 83(b) filings** (B, C, D). C states the underlying tax constraint most precisely (§83: equity for past services is compensation income at FMV — so the only cheap moment is now). **Plan A largely misses this issue** — a substantive gap given that A's design contains a full retroactive backfill.
15. **Double-trigger acceleration as the default; no broad single-trigger** (A, B, D; C allows a modest 4-tranche single-trigger on sale for active founders and flags it as negotiable — a mild, disclosed deviation).
16. **Forfeited/unearned founder equity flows to the shared contribution pool** (A, B, C) — Plan D routes forfeitures to a neutral company buffer instead, with a genuinely good reason (see Divergence #5).

### Consensus on the evidence itself (A, B, C — the citing plans)

All three citing plans independently converge on the same epistemic map, which this audit confirms:
- The Hellmann/Wasserman "First Deal" findings (≈⅓ equal splits; 73% split in the first month; quick-handshake penalty ≈10% of equity/$450K NPV) are real but **correlational — the authors themselves attribute it to selection**. All three carry the caveat. (Verified.)
- Wasserman 2017 (*SMJ*): 6,130 startups, each level of retained founder control associated with **17.1–22.0% lower pre-money valuation** — verified exactly; all three label it correlational.
- The famous "65% of startup failures are people problems" figure has **no peer-reviewed provenance**; all three citing plans independently flag it as directional/book-level. (Good behavior, uniformly.)
- **No peer-reviewed empirical validation of Slicing Pie or any dynamic-equity framework exists.** All three say so explicitly and manage the risk with the freeze. This shared honesty is a notable strength of the whole exercise.

**What this shows:** B and C carry nearly all of the legal load; A carries the packaging, calibration, and negotiation-craft load; D contributes two things nobody else has (conflict-of-interest hygiene on forfeitures; a serious cost-of-complexity critique) and omits nearly everything else.

### Issues every plan missed

1. **Workers' compensation and payroll-adjacent registration.** Massachusetts requires workers' comp coverage for employees; a 15-person shop that has never run payroll almost certainly has never carried it, and the same remediation that fixes wages must fix this. No plan mentions it.
2. **Immigration exposure of unpaid workers.** If any of the ~15 people is on a status with work restrictions, a year of unpaid "employment" is its own violation independent of the Wage Act. Nobody asks.
3. **The interim period.** Every plan describes the end state; none states what governs during weeks 0–10 while counsel drafts — during which unpaid work presumably continues and the violations keep accruing. A's "2-page MOU" gestures at it but no plan drafts interim terms or addresses whether work should pause or wage-comply *immediately* rather than at signing.
4. **Cash to fund compliance.** B says wage payments are "senior to all other uses of cash" and C says "budget it into any raise," but no plan models the actual monthly cost of minimum-wage-compliant payroll for 15 people (~$40–50k/month at $15/hr full-time) or proposes the bridge (founder loans, a small SAFE) that makes remediation real rather than aspirational.
5. **Post-round dilution modeling.** No plan shows the founders what the structures produce *after* a seed round (≈20% dilution + 10–15% pool refresh) — the number the founders will actually care about when deciding. C's calibration section comes closest.
6. **Benefits cliff for employees becoming K-1 partners** (health insurance eligibility, §125 plans). B names the issue in a flag; no plan addresses what the ten employees are told they lose and what replaces it.

## 5. VERDICT

### Ranking

**1. Plan B (branch `plan-1`).** The most correct and most complete document set. It is the only plan that covers the full legal surface (Wage Act with accurate primary authority, securities law, §36/§32, no-statutory-expulsion, tax safe harbors with current case law, the complete Yale suite), and its evidence discipline is the best in the set — a working strength taxonomy, honest flags that this audit repeatedly validated (Miele, secondary medians, FOA empirics), and accurate descriptions of what each source actually holds (*Segal* is the test case). Its worked mechanics reconcile arithmetically. Deductions: the 5-seat board contradicts its own cited norms; the quarterly-issuance-with-fresh-83(b)-rounds machinery is the heaviest operational design of the four and the plan under-weighs that cost; one wrong (but flagged) reporter cite; the performance gate on set-asides is defensible but weaker-argued than A's contrary position.

**2. Plan C (branch `plan-2`).** Nearly B's equal in legal depth and its superior on tax strategy (the §83 nominal-FMV timing argument and the early-conversion analysis are the two best individual pieces of reasoning in the entire exercise), on the professor's *legal* posture (indefiniteness/quantum meruit/settlement-and-release/Fenn), and on quantified transparency (the only full 15-person worked example, which recomputes exactly). Deductions: the two citation errors found in this audit (the misattributed 65% statistic — the only disqualified finding in the set — and the Reuter miscite); the quality factor reintroduces the subjectivity every plan otherwise engineers out; no ledger backstop date; securities law honestly but consequentially out of scope.

**3. Plan A (branch `claude/startup-governance-equity-8webj5`).** The best-packaged and most investor-*presentable* plan — the one-pager, 90-day roadmap, negotiation-reality notes, and merit-formula document are the artifacts a founder team would actually sign and a seed investor would actually read — and its research briefs show real epistemic care. But it is the weakest of the three citing plans on the things that would actually hurt this company: the Wage Act is a late footnote rather than the first workstream, the retroactive grant's tax problem is unaddressed, securities compliance is absent, and its legal sourcing leans on tertiary websites. It also keeps the shotgun (against the better evidence) and contains one internal inconsistency (rate-spread claim vs. its own Chief Scientist row). Strong specificity, mid correctness on law, good honesty.

**4. Plan D (branch `claude/founder-governance-equity-inactivity-sgxk74`).** A fraction of the others' depth, zero citations, and it quietly rewrites the client's stated requirements (no per-founder 5% set-asides as specified; employees excluded from identical terms). Ranked last on correctness-versus-brief and evidentiary transparency — but it is not noise: its conflict-of-interest argument for buffer-routed forfeitures, its notice→cure→pause→forfeit inactivity ladder, and its cost-of-complexity critique of live ledgers are each better than the corresponding piece of the winning plans, and all of its uncited legal claims turned out to be accurate.

### Recommended synthesis

**Base: Plan B**, restructured as follows:

1. **Shrink the board to 3 seats** (CEO ex officio, one elected active-founder seat, independent chair with the casting vote) — adopting A/C/D's position and B's own cited norms. Keep B's observer rights for off-board ≥2% holders and B's Contribution Committee.
2. **Keep B's legal spine intact**: Wage Act remediation as the gating workstream (merge C's *Cook v. Patient Edu* and *Koshy* cites and C's "equity cannot settle wage claims — sequence the releases" instruction); profits-interest mechanics with ES NPA comfort; Rule 701/blue-sky section; §36/§32 handling; no-statutory-expulsion article; *Miele*-route forfeiture covenants (with the corrected cite, 496 Mass. 171).
3. **Import C's tax strategy wholesale**: settle the retroactive year immediately inside the nominal-FMV window (contemporaneous valuation, Form 15620/83(b) within 30 days), and **decide conversion timing as an explicit founder decision** with C's analysis as the default recommendation (convert early — within ~2 quarters — unless the founders want the ledger to run longer, in which case B's convert-at-financing with the 36-month backstop governs). Import C's linear partial-pay interpolation into the multiplier and C's settlement-and-release structure (with 60-day window) for the professor.
4. **Import A's operator-facing layer**: the one-pager format and 90-day roadmap; the ratification negotiation guidance (carrots held in reserve; cliff-credit as a concession, not a default); the backfill protocol (default-hours convention, "negotiate the activity timeline, not hour counts" sensitivity point); the ex-ante quarterly role-split assignment as the primary anti-gaming device; and the leadership-ownership floor check (~30% CEO+top-2 at freeze) before any round.
5. **Import D's two mechanisms**: route forfeited *set-aside* tranches to a neutral unissued buffer (not to the pool the certifiers share in), or at minimum present pool-vs-buffer as a flagged decision with the conflict-of-interest tradeoff stated; and adopt D's notice → 30-day cure → pause → 6-month forfeit sequence in place of B's terser deemed-separation clause.
6. **Drop from the base**: B's quarterly performance gate on set-aside tranches (replace with A's time-only earning plus role-reassignment for underperformance — or keep B's deemed-met version only if the founders insist on a performance condition); C's quality factor (rely on role assignment + rate leveling instead); any shotgun mechanism (already absent from B).
7. **Add what everyone missed** (§4): workers' comp and payroll registration in the remediation workstream; an interim-period instruction (wage compliance starts now, not at signing); a one-page cash plan for compliant payroll; a post-seed dilution model in the founder-facing deck; a written benefits-transition note for the ten employees.

The result would be a plan with B's correctness, C's tax and settlement strategy, A's signability, and D's procedural hygiene — which none of the four achieves alone.
