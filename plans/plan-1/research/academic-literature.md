# Research Brief: Founder Equity Splits, Team Dynamics, and Startup Governance (Academic Literature)

## 1. Wasserman / Hellmann & Wasserman — Equity split research

**Hellmann & Wasserman, "The First Deal: The Division of Founder Equity in New Ventures," *Management Science* 63(8): 2647–2666 (2016; NBER WP 16922, 2011).** The definitive empirical study. Data: proprietary CompStudy surveys of US tech and life-science startups — early version 1,476 founders in 511 ventures; later HBS working-paper version (14-085, 2014) covers 3,782 founders in 1,367 companies. Key findings:

- **~32–33% of founding teams split equity exactly equally** — far more than a contribution-based model would predict.
- **42% of teams were "quick handshake" splitters** — agreeing in one day or less; **73% of teams split equity within the first month** of founding, when uncertainty about roles, commitment, and contributions is highest. Equal splits strongly correlate with quick, negotiation-avoiding processes.
- **Consequences:** equal-splitting and quick-handshake teams are less likely to raise outside financing and receive lower average valuations when they do. The authors estimate stronger founders who accept an equal split leave ~10% of firm equity / ~25% of the average founder stake / **~$450K NPV** "on the table." Important caveat the authors themselves stress: the fundraising penalty appears to be **selection, not causation** — inequality-averse ("egalitarian") teams differ systematically from teams willing to negotiate.
- **Renegotiation:** in principle splits can be renegotiated; in practice obstacles are severe (unanimity required; the over-allocated founder has no incentive to concede). About **half of teams in Wasserman's data had no dynamic elements at all** (no vesting, buyout, or adjustment terms) — a purely static handshake.

**Wasserman, *The Founder's Dilemmas* (Princeton University Press, 2012).** Based on ~10,000 founders / ~3,600+ startups (CompStudy panel, 2000–2009) plus case studies. Core equity-related arguments: teams avoid the "elephant in the room" negotiation to preserve harmony, imposing costs later; he advocates discussing contingencies up front and using vesting. Also home of the "rich vs. king" tradeoff, formalized in **Wasserman, "The Throne vs. the Kingdom," *SMJ* 38(2): 255–277 (2017)** — on 6,130 US startups, each level of founder control retained (board control and/or CEO seat) is associated with a **17.1–22.0% lower pre-money valuation**, especially after year three. Evidence: strong sample, but survey-based and correlational.

## 2. Founder conflict as a failure cause

- **Wasserman's 65% figure**: from his HBS dataset — **65% of high-potential startups fail because of co-founder conflict / "people problems"** (vs. 35% product/market/functional). Widely quoted (Entrepreneur, CNN Money, HBS materials). Flag: this comes from his analysis of failed firms in his sample, not a separate peer-reviewed failure study; the precise denominator and coding are not published in a refereed article — treat as credible expert estimate, not a hard number.
- **CB Insights startup post-mortems** (recurring report, 2014–2021, 101–300+ post-mortems): "**not the right team**" cited in **~23%** of failures (14% in the 2021 revision); "disharmony among team/investors" appears separately (~13% in early versions). Top causes were market-related: no market need (~35–42%), ran out of cash (~29–38%), outcompeted (~19–20%). Flag: self-reported post-mortems, multiple causes per firm, non-random sample — directionally useful only. Note the tension: CB Insights puts team issues well below market issues, while Wasserman's 65% counts underlying people dynamics rather than proximate causes. Both can be true; they measure different things.
- **Breugst, Patzelt & Rathgeber, "How Should We Divide the Pie?" *JBV* 30(1): 66–94 (2015)**: longitudinal multiple-case study of 8 entrepreneurial teams over 6 months. **Perceived justice of the equity split** (not equality per se) drives team dynamics: high perceived justice → positive interaction spirals; low perceived justice → negative spirals; external threats can flip even just-perceiving teams into negative spirals. Small-N qualitative — strong mechanism evidence, no statistical generalizability.

## 3. Dynamic vs. static splits, renegotiation, vesting

- **Hellmann & Thiele, "Contracting Among Founders" (SSRN 2012; later *JLEO*)**: formal theory of early vs. delayed founder contracting. Trade-off: early commitment risks locking in ineffective founders; delay resolves uncertainty about relative skill but risks idea appropriation/defection. Delay is more attractive when skill uncertainty is high — theoretical support for waiting or building in contingencies.
- **Vesting as commitment device**: Wasserman (2012) argues vesting (typically 4-year/1-year cliff) converts a static split into a semi-dynamic one and protects against early departure; his data show roughly half of teams omit any such terms. Empirical literature on vesting is mostly about investor-imposed vesting (Kaplan & Strömberg, *RES* 2003, VC contracts) rather than founder-to-founder vesting. **Peer-reviewed evidence on dynamic-split frameworks (e.g., Moyer's "Slicing Pie," 2012) is essentially absent** — practitioner literature only. Flag: this is the thinnest evidence area; academics acknowledge contingent agreements as an open research question.

## 4. Founding team size

- **Klotz, Hmieleski, Bradley & Busenitz, "New Venture Teams," *Journal of Management* 40(1): 226–255 (2014)**: definitive literature review (IMO framework). Average new venture team ~2–3 members; team-founded ventures generally outperform solo-founded on growth/survival in prior studies, but size effects are curvilinear/contingent — benefits of added human capital are offset by coordination and conflict costs.
- **Ruef, Aldrich & Carter, *American Sociological Review* 68 (2003)** (and Ruef, *The Entrepreneurial Group*, 2010, PSED representative data): founding teams form by **homophily and strong ties**, not competence complementarity — most teams involve family, friends, or coworkers (14–17% former co-workers, 19–21% friends/acquaintances); teams are far more homogeneous than chance would predict. Implication: composition mechanisms predispose teams to relationship-based conflict.
- **Kotha & George, *JBV* 27(5): 525–543 (2012)**: experienced founders retain more equity and share more selectively with helpers; novices give away more equity, affecting resource mobilization.
- **Choi, Goldschlag, Haltiwanger & Kim, "Founding Teams and Startup Performance" (NBER WP 28417 / Census WP 19-32)**: US administrative data; premature death of a founding-team member causes large, persistent declines in startup size, survival, and productivity — causal evidence that founding-team human capital matters, strongest in small teams.
- **On 4–5 founder teams specifically**: no robust study identifies an "optimal" number. Hellmann & Wasserman find larger teams negotiate less quickly and agree less easily; equal splits are more common in 2-person teams. Free-rider concerns in bigger teams rest mostly on general teams research (social loafing / Steiner's process losses), not startup-specific causal evidence. Flag: thin.

## 5. Agency vs. stewardship theory; early-stage boards

- **Donaldson & Davis (1991, *Australian Journal of Management*)** ground stewardship theory (executives as trustworthy stewards; duality can help) vs. agency theory (monitoring, separation of roles).
- **Arthurs & Busenitz (2003, *ETP*), "Boundaries and Limitations of Agency Theory and Stewardship Theory in the VC/Entrepreneur Relationship"**: neither theory alone fits startups; founders' psychological ownership makes pure agency assumptions misleading.
- **Garg, "Venture Boards: Distinctive Monitoring and Implications for Firm Performance," *AMR* 38(1): 90–108 (2013)** and **Garg & Furr (*SEJ*, 2017)**: venture boards differ fundamentally from public boards — VC directors are powerful, incentivized principals-as-monitors; monitoring is intertwined with resource provision. **Garg (2020)** even inverts agency roles (CEO as principal, directors as agents). Related: research on CEO duality in ventures suggests duality can be value-enhancing early (stewardship logic) — see *Journal of Business Venturing Insights* (2022) on venture governance and CEO duality. Fried, Bruton & Hisrich (1998, *JBV*) found VC-backed boards more active than others. Evidence: theory-heavy; empirical work growing but modest.

## 6. Contribution/performance-based equity allocation

Direct peer-reviewed tests are scarce. What exists: Hellmann & Wasserman (2016) show splits deviate from equality when founders differ in idea generation, capital contribution, and prior founding experience (seniority/idea-provider premia); Kotha & George (2012) on contribution- and experience-driven allocation; Breugst et al. (2015) show *perceived* contribution-fairness, not formulaic accuracy, is what stabilizes teams; Hellmann & Thiele (2012) provide the theory for contingent/deferred allocation. No published RCT or large-sample evaluation of formula-based dynamic models (Slicing Pie etc.). Flag: thin — a genuine evidence gap.

**Overall strength summary**: Strongest — Hellmann & Wasserman split data, Wasserman throne/kingdom, Choi et al. causal team evidence, Klotz et al. review. Moderate — Ruef, Kotha & George, Garg governance theory. Thin — the 65% conflict figure's provenance, CB Insights (non-academic), dynamic-equity frameworks, optimal team size, and contribution-based allocation outcomes.
