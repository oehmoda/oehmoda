# Research Brief: Governance & Decision-Making for a 4–5 Founder Early-Stage Company (with MA LLC Focus)

## 1. Board structure by stage

Industry consensus (NYU Entrepreneurship, Rho, Shockwave Innovations, dot.LA): **Pre-seed:** founders hold all seats — typically a 1–3 person board (CEO plus one co-founder; occasionally an angel). Do NOT put all 4–5 founders on the board; even-numbered and oversized boards invite deadlock. **Seed:** 3 seats — 2 founders + 1 investor (2:1 founder control). **Series A:** 5 seats — 2 founders, 2 investors, 1 independent director (2:2:1), now the standard; the independent seat is usually mandated in the Series A term sheet and serves as tiebreaker. Advice: recruit a credible independent early so investors accept your pick; negotiate board structure "before you need to" because seats compound through rounds.

**For LLCs:** UpCounsel and practitioner guides confirm LLCs can create a **board of managers** in the operating agreement that mirrors a corporate board — the agreement defines seat count, appointment/removal rights, quorum, and which decisions are board-level vs. reserved to members (equity holders). Typical split: board handles strategy, budgets, officer appointment; members vote on fundamental matters (amendments, dissolution, mergers, new members).

## 2. Decision-making frameworks and reserved matters

The ABA LLC Agreement Checklist and LegalGPS/LegalClarity materials describe a standard **tiered voting structure**:

- **Manager/CEO discretion:** ordinary-course operations, hiring below executive level, spending within an approved budget (often with a dollar threshold, e.g., contracts > $25–50K escalate).
- **Simple majority (of board or units):** annual budget, executive hires/fires, opening credit lines, settling minor litigation.
- **Supermajority (commonly 66.7%, 75%, or 80%):** admitting new members, issuing new units, debt above a threshold, related-party transactions, changing distributions, removing a manager, sale of material assets.
- **Unanimous:** amending the operating agreement's fundamental economic/class rights, dissolution, selling company IP, converting entity form. Practitioner warning (NY Business Divorce blog): keep the unanimous list *short* — long unanimity lists hand every founder a veto and manufacture deadlock.

**The "one CEO" principle:** Wasserman's *Founder's Dilemmas* research (212+ startups; HBR "The Founder's Dilemma," 2008) frames the **rich-vs-king tradeoff**: founders who maximize control build less valuable companies; those who cede control/equity capture an estimated 80–100% value premium. Point Nine ("Can a startup have two CEOs?") and Entrepreneur's co-CEO pieces: investors "laugh you out of the room" at co-CEOs; consensus models slow decisions precisely when speed is the startup's only advantage. Ben Horowitz (a16z "Peacetime CEO/Wartime CEO," *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*): a CEO who waits for everyone to agree adds no value — someone must decide with imperfect information. Elad Gil ("Unequal Cofounders"): truly shared power makes startups less aggressive because everything becomes a negotiated compromise. YC data: ~20% of YC startups lose a founder; founder fights are on PG's "18 Mistakes That Kill Startups" list. **Concrete recommendation:** designate one CEO with day-to-day authority; other founders get functional authority in their domains; a short reserved-matters list is the check on the CEO — not day-to-day co-decision rights.

## 3. Founder roles and titles (4–5 founders)

CRV and Pilot's Tactical Guide: divide by **real strength, not default titles** (DoorDash launched with 4 co-founders this way). Common 4–5 founder pattern: CEO (external: fundraising, sales, hiring), CTO (technical vision), COO (internal ops) as third title, then CPO/Chief Scientist/VP Eng. CofounderFit's research on conflict: most co-founder conflict starts as "quiet overlap" — two people think they own product, nobody owns sales — so write a **DRI (directly responsible individual) map**: every function has exactly one owner. Avoid title inflation: a founder taking "CTO" or "COO" at 5 people may be layered later; several guides recommend "Head of X" titles initially to preserve room to hire above. **Scaling transitions** (HSG, RBCx, JRG Partners): the signal a founder role has outgrown its holder is when the founder becomes the bottleneck (all hires/decisions wait on them). Best practice: agree *in advance* (ideally in the founder agreement) that titles are role assignments, not property; a founder replaced as an executive can remain as board member, CPO, chairman, or IC — and Wasserman found 4 of 5 founders resist this transition, which is why pre-commitment matters.

## 4. Massachusetts LLC operating agreement essentials

Under **M.G.L. c. 156C**: the operating agreement can be written or oral (§2(9)), but §11 good-faith-reliance protection requires a *written* agreement — always write it. Key provisions for a 5-founder MA LLC:

- **Manager-managed** structure strongly preferred over member-managed with 5 founders: name 1–3 managers (or a board of managers), so the entity isn't run by 5-way member vote; also cleaner for future investors.
- **Fiduciary duties:** §63 expressly allows duties/liabilities of members and managers to be "expanded or restricted" in the agreement (Romano Law; Boston Bar Journal notes MA close-corporation fiduciary duties under *Donahue* are otherwise strict, so express modification — e.g., permitting outside activities, defining corporate-opportunity carve-outs — is valuable).
- **Transfer restrictions:** blanket prohibition on transfers without consent, plus **ROFR** (company then members may match third-party offers), **tag-along** (minority may join a majority sale on identical terms), **drag-along** (supermajority can force all members into an approved company sale — investors will require this) (Thomson Reuters; LegalGPS).
- **Deadlock resolution** (Jacobs Law (MA firm), Crowley Law, LegalClarity): tiered escalation — (1) mandatory negotiation/cooling-off, (2) mediation (89% commercial settlement rates cited), (3) binding arbitration or a **casting vote** by a designated tiebreaker/independent, (4) buy-sell of last resort: **shotgun clause** (offeror names one price, other side buys or sells at it — self-fairness incentive, but favors the cash-rich party) or **Texas shootout** (sealed bids; highest bidder buys). Define what triggers "deadlock" precisely.
- **Expulsion/buyout:** for-cause removal (fraud, breach, felony, abandonment) by supermajority of *other* members, with a valuation formula (appraisal or pre-agreed multiple) and payment terms; pair with **founder vesting** of units so a departing founder keeps only vested equity — the Zipcar lesson.
- **Non-compete/IP:** all founders sign IP assignment + confidentiality (the Snapchat lesson). MA Noncompetition Agreement Act (G.L. c. 149 §24L, post-Oct 2018) requires garden leave (~50% salary) and a 12-month cap for *employment* noncompetes — but **exempts noncompetes tied to sale of a business by significant owners** (Beck Reed Riden); draft founder noncompetes tied to equity ownership/sale, not employment.

## 5. Founder performance measurement

Doerr, *Measure What Matters*: company OKRs start at the top; each founder/executive carries personal quarterly OKRs; red flag when "the CEO says all my team goals are my goals." Replace annual reviews with **CFRs** (Conversations, Feedback, Recognition) — continuous, decoupled from compensation. Applied to founders: quarterly OKR reviews before the full founder group or board; annual 360/peer review of each founder; the board (or the other members) formally reviews the CEO annually. Tie continued **equity vesting to time, not OKR attainment** (metric-gated vesting invites gaming), but tie *role retention* to performance: pre-agree that persistent underperformance in a function is grounds for role reassignment by board/majority vote. Horowitz adds: judge executives on output of their organization, and "look for the exceptional, not the absence of weakness."

## 6. Canonical founder-conflict case studies

- **Zipcar (Robin Chase/Antje Danielson):** 50/50 "handshake" split before contributions were known; Danielson never went full-time yet kept her stake. Chase: "a really stupid handshake." Cited (incl. by Wasserman and Eric Ries) for: **never split equity before roles/commitment are clear, and always use vesting**.
- **Facebook/Eduardo Saverin:** early ~30% stake diluted below 10% via restructuring after Saverin stayed in NY and froze accounts; litigation settled. Cited for: **vesting schedules, defined roles/commitment levels, and written rights around dilution and removal** — plus fiduciary-duty risk when diluting a co-founder.
- **Snapchat/Reggie Brown:** idea originator pushed out with nothing in writing; sued claiming jointly-owned IP; **$157.5M settlement** (disclosed in Snap's IPO filing). Cited for: **execute IP assignments and founder agreements on day one; document who is a founder and what they own before value exists**.
