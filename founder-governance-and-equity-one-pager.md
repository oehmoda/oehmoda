# Governance & Equity for a 4–5 Founder Startup — One-Pager

*How modern successful startups govern and share equity: research-backed best practices, tailored to a 1-year-old, 5-founder Massachusetts LLC with ~10 employees. Full research notes with sources in [`research/`](research/).*

---

## What the evidence says

- **People problems, not product problems, kill founding teams.** Wasserman (HBS, ~10,000 founders) attributes ~65% of high-potential startup failures to co-founder conflict; ~20% of YC startups lose a founder. The #1 predictor of trouble is exactly your situation: a "quick handshake" split made before contributions were knowable (42% of teams decide in a day; Hellmann & Wasserman, *Management Science* 2016).
- **Static splits without vesting are the canonical mistake.** Half of founding teams have no vesting/buyback terms at all; renegotiation later almost never works because the over-allocated founder holds a veto. Zipcar, Facebook/Saverin, and Snapchat ($157.5M settlement) are all failures of exactly this.
- **Perceived fairness — not equality — stabilizes teams** (Breugst et al., *JBV* 2015). Equal splits work when contributions stay comparable; when they diverge, what matters is a *process* everyone accepts as just.
- **Control concentrated in one CEO outperforms consensus.** Founders who insist on shared control build less valuable companies (Wasserman's "rich vs. king": 17–22% lower valuations per level of control retained). Investors reject co-CEO structures outright.

## Governance: one CEO, small board, tiered decision rights

1. **Convert to a manager-managed LLC with a 3-person board of managers** (not 5). Recommended: CEO + one other active founder + one independent the founders jointly pick. Odd number prevents deadlock; 5-way member management is unworkable and unfundable.
2. **Designate exactly one CEO** with day-to-day authority. Other founders hold functional roles with real authority in their domain — checked by the board, not by co-deciding everything.
3. **Tiered decision rights in the operating agreement:** CEO discretion for ordinary course (with a spending threshold, e.g., >$25–50K goes to the board) → board majority for budgets, executive hires, litigation → member supermajority (e.g., 75% of units) for new members, new unit issuance, major debt, manager removal, asset sales → unanimity *only* for amending fundamental economic rights and dissolution. Keep the unanimous list short — every item on it is a veto.
4. **Deadlock ladder:** mandatory negotiation → mediation → independent casting vote → buy-sell (shotgun/Texas-shootout) as last resort. Define "deadlock" precisely.

## Founder roles

- **One owner per function (DRI map).** Most founder conflict starts as quiet overlap. Write down who owns product, engineering, sales, ops, finance — exactly one name each.
- **Titles are role assignments, not property.** Pre-commit in writing that the board can reassign roles for sustained underperformance, and that a founder replaced as an executive can stay as a member/board observer. This is the clause everyone wishes they had signed on day one.

## Equity: two layers, everything vests

Your existing informal deal (guaranteed founder base + contribution-based remainder) is actually a sound *dynamic* structure — it just needs mechanics:

1. **Layer 1 — Founder base (fixed):** a guaranteed allocation per founder (e.g., 8–10% each, ~40–50% total) honoring the original partnership. Research favors generosity here: motivation from fair treatment beats points saved.
2. **Layer 2 — Contribution pool (dynamic):** the remainder allocated by a Slicing Pie–style formula until a freeze event. Each person's share = their at-risk contributions ÷ everyone's: unpaid time at fair-market rate ×2, cash ×4, valued quarterly and recorded. This is the only widely used framework that objectively handles "some employees contribute more than some founders" — employees earn from the same pool by the same formula. **Freeze the pie** (percentages become fixed) at profitability/self-sufficiency or a priced financing round.
3. **All units vest: 4-year monthly, 1-year cliff, with retroactive credit** for the year already served. Vesting — not split haggling — is the industry-standard protection against inactivity. Add double-trigger acceleration on acquisition.
4. **Inactive founders:** unvested units are forfeited/repurchased at cost automatically; add good-leaver/bad-leaver terms (bad leaver = cause, breach, abandonment → buyback at cost; good leaver → keeps vested, company ROFR at fair value). Resolve current inactivity *now* — investors treat >10–15% "dead equity" as a diligence red flag that delays or kills rounds.
5. **Employees:** grant **profits interests** (the LLC analog of options — tax-free at grant with a valuation hurdle, per Rev. Proc. 93-27) from a **10–15% pool**, 4yr/1yr-cliff vesting. Benchmarks: ~1.5–2% for a first key hire, <5% total across the first 10 (Carta 2024).

## Performance measurement

Quarterly **founder OKRs** reviewed in front of the full founder group; annual **360 peer review** of each founder and board review of the CEO. Tie **vesting to time** (metric-gated vesting invites gaming) but tie **role retention and the contribution pool** to measured performance — that's where accountability belongs.

## Massachusetts-specific notes for the lawyer

Write the operating agreement (oral is legal under M.G.L. c. 156C but forfeits §11 protections); use **§63** to expressly tailor fiduciary duties (outside-activity and corporate-opportunity carve-outs — MA default duties are strict); include ROFR + tag-along + drag-along transfer restrictions; structure founder noncompetes as **tied to equity ownership/sale**, not employment (exempt from the 2018 MA Noncompete Act's garden-leave requirement); and execute **IP assignments from all founders and employees immediately**.
