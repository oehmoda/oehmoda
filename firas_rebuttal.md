# A Note to My Cofounders: Let's Make Our Cap Table Something Investors Want to Buy

*From Firas. Please read this before we sign anything. Five minutes.*

## Start from the goal

We plan to raise money. When investors look at us, they don't just judge the product — **they judge the ownership table**. They've seen thousands of startups, they know what winning ones look like at seed, and they pattern-match hard. Our equity plan is excellent in almost every way investors care about: everything vests, inactive founders lose their unearned shares automatically, and every percent is backed by documented work. Those are genuine selling points.

But the plan has one gap that can undo all of that — and it's fixable today, for free.

## The gap

- Each founder gets **up to 5%** set aside (25% total). This is a **ceiling, not a promise** — you earn it month by month, only while actively working, and whatever isn't earned flows into the shared pool.
- The other **75%** is earned by founders *and* ~10 employees under identical rules. Ten full-time employees will log more hours than three to five founders. More hours = more shares.
- So **nothing in the plan controls where the founders' total lands.** It could be 60%. It could be 40%. Nobody can compute it today, because it depends on a work ledger we haven't built yet.

## What investors expect to see at seed

- The **median founding team owns 56.2%** after their seed round ([Carta, 45,000+ startups](https://carta.com/data/founder-ownership-2026/)); guidance for *entering* seed is founders around [70%](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/founder-ownership-by-round), and [at least ~50% through the seed round](https://www.crv.com/content/startup-equity-structure).
- The **median employee pool at seed is 12.1%** ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/founder-ownership-2026/)); even the most employee-generous mainstream advice ([YC-style](https://mr-p.medium.com/how-big-should-your-option-pool-be-5164e6cfcc0)) tops out around 20%. Our plan could plausibly produce 25–35% employee ownership — far outside any pattern investors recognize.
- Diligence **deal-killers** include founders owning less than investors combined and large stakes held by inactive people ([Startup Law Blog](https://www.thestartuplawblog.com/cap-table-management-founders-guide/), [Equity Matrix](https://equitymatrix.io/blog/what-investors-look-for-in-cap-tables)); VCs want departed founders down at [single digits](https://www.saastr.com/how-vcs-think-about-departed-co-founder-equity-stakes/).
- The reason is simple: investors are buying **7–10 more years of founder obsession**. A founder team with a thin stake reads as a team that might not stay hungry ([YC/Seibel](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/how-to-split-equity-among-founders)).

An unusual cap table doesn't always kill a deal — but it means every investor meeting starts with us explaining our ownership instead of our product, and it prices in as risk. A standard-looking cap table is free marketing; a strange one is a permanent tax on every raise.

## Why this is good for everyone — including the employees

This is not founders versus employees. If the cap table blocks or discounts our fundraise, **everyone's shares are worth less** — the employees' most of all, since their equity only becomes money if the company can raise, grow, and exit. Twenty percent of a funded, growing company beats thirty-five percent of one that can't raise. Keeping the company fundable *is* the pro-employee position. And under any fix we'd adopt, employees still earn under identical rules and still end up far above the 12% market norm — a genuine recruiting story no normal startup can offer.

## Objections you might raise — and my honest answers

**"You're speculating. Founders might end up above 50% anyway."**
Maybe! That's the point — *nobody knows*. If the projection comes out fine, a floor costs nothing and changes nothing. If it comes out low, the floor is the difference between a fundable company and a broken one. There is no scenario where the floor hurts us.

**"A median is not a rule — great traction raises anyway."**
True, exceptions exist. But exceptions pay for their cap table with extra traction. Why volunteer to need an excuse, when today the fix is a one-line parameter?

**"Our own research says founder control is linked to lower valuations."**
That finding (Wasserman 2017) is about hoarding *control* — board seats and the CEO chair — not share economics, and it's correlational. No study anywhere says investors prefer founders with thin economics; the practitioner evidence says the opposite.

**"Changing this now is unfair to the employees."**
Nothing is signed yet — this is the designed moment to set parameters, and our plan itself says to agree the rules before computing anyone's numbers. A floor changes the pool's *size*, not its rules: same formula, same fairness, still the most employee-generous plan in the market.

**"If founders end up thin, we fix it at the round with top-up grants."**
Top-ups come from the new option pool, at the investors' discretion, taxed as compensation — and they require renegotiating with the very people who just out-earned us. The research is blunt: renegotiation after signing almost never works ([Hellmann & Wasserman, *Management Science* 2016](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2474)). A floor written today needs no one's permission later.

## What I'm asking for

1. **Run the backfill projection before we ratify.** Build the past-year ledger the plan already specifies and look at the projected ownership table. Decide with facts.
2. **Adopt an investor-readiness target now: founders collectively in the 50–60% zone at the seed round** — the market-median position. If the projection lands below it, we adjust the set-aside/pool ratio *before* signing, while it's a parameter and not a renegotiation.

I like this plan. The earn-it structure is right, it's honest, and it's our promise to the team. I just want us to walk into our seed round looking like the companies that get funded — instead of explaining why we don't.
