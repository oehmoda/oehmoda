# Research Brief: Founder Equity, Vesting, and Employee Equity — Industry Best Practices

## 1. Y Combinator: the case for equal splits and standard vesting

**Source: Michael Seibel (YC Managing Director), "How to Split Equity Among Co-Founders," michaelseibel.com / YC Startup Library (~2019).** Seibel argues nearly all startups should split equity **equally or near-equally**, on four grounds: (a) building a valuable company takes **7–10 years**, so small differences in year-one contribution (who had the idea, who started a few months earlier) don't justify large permanent equity gaps; (b) **more equity = more motivation**, and a highly motivated co-founder is worth far more than the few points you "saved"; (c) heavily lopsided splits (e.g., 90/10) signal the CEO doesn't view the co-founder as a true partner — "if you aren't willing to give your partner an equal share, perhaps you are choosing the wrong partner"; (d) startups fail most often from founder breakups, and resentment over unfair splits compounds during hard times. YC's standard advice pairs this with **4-year vesting, 1-year cliff** for all founders (25% at month 12, then 1/48 monthly), which converts "who deserves what" into "who stays and keeps earning." YC views vesting — not an unequal split — as the correct protection against a founder leaving early.

## 2. Counter-arguments and the Slicing Pie dynamic model

**Dan Shapiro (Glowforge CEO), "The Only Wrong Answer is 50/50," danshapiro.com / GeekWire, 2011.** Shapiro argues equal splits are usually the result of conflict-avoidance, not fairness. His heuristic: start at equal, then adjust — roughly **+5% for the original idea**, a premium for the **CEO role** (market rate for CEOs exceeds CTOs; and someone must be decisively in charge), more for **full-time vs. part-time** founders (they bear more risk), adjustments for **cash invested** (treat as a convertible-note-like contribution rather than raw equity where possible), and for reputation/experience that materially de-risks fundraising. He also argues a 50/50 split creates governance deadlock.

**Mike Moyer, *Slicing Pie* / *The Slicing Pie Handbook* (2012; handbook 2016), slicingpie.com.** A **dynamic** equity model (the "Grunt Fund") for bootstrapped startups: your % = your share of total at-risk contributions, recalculated continuously, so the split is never "wrong." Mechanics:

- Every unpaid, at-risk contribution converts to **slices**: Slices = **fair market value × multiplier**. Standard multipliers: **non-cash 2x** (time at fair-market rate minus any salary paid, ideas, relationships, pre-owned equipment) and **cash 4x** (money spent, unreimbursed expenses). (Some practitioners run 1x/2x; Moyer's canonical numbers are 2x/4x, rewarding cash's higher scarcity/risk.) Example: covering $1,000 of expenses = 4,000 slices; a developer with a $150,000 market salary working unpaid contributes ~$75/hour × 2 in slices.
- Your equity at any moment = your slices ÷ all slices.
- **Recovery framework** (what happens on departure): if a person **resigns without good reason or is fired for cause**, they forfeit non-cash slices entirely; cash contributions are recovered at **face value, no multiplier** (buyback at 1x if the company can afford it), and a non-compete applies. If **terminated without cause or resigning for good reason**, they keep all slices including multipliers and are released from non-competes — a built-in severance/retention mechanism.
- **Freezing the pie**: the model terminates and percentages become fixed at **breakeven/self-sufficiency or a Series A** (i.e., when contributions are no longer at risk because people are paid market rates).
- Critique worth noting: startup lawyers (e.g., **Silicon Hills Lawyer, "Splitting Founder Equity: Avoid Grunt Funds," 2015**) argue dynamic models create legal/tax complexity (constant re-grants, 83(b) issues in corporations) and that institutional VCs dislike them; Slicing Pie works best pre-financing, especially in LLCs.

## 3. Carta and Index Ventures data

**Carta ("How do co-founders actually split equity?"; Founder Ownership Report 2025, Peter Walker):** In **2024, 45.9% of two-founder teams split equally**, up from **31.5% in 2015**; the median two-founder split has moved from ~60/40 to **51/49**. For **three-founder teams**, equal splits rose from **12.1% to 26.9%** (2015–2024). So a slight majority of teams still split unequally, but the trend is strongly toward equality — converging on YC's advice. **Option pools:** typical **10–15% at seed/Series A**, growing to **~18% by Series C**; pools are usually created/refreshed pre-money at each round (dilution borne by existing holders). **Employee grants** (8,000+ first-10-hire grants, 2023–24): first hire median **~1.5%** (25th pct 0.5%, 75th pct 4.0%); median first **engineering** hire ~2.0%; by hire #5 median ~0.33%; most companies use **<5% total for their first 10 hires**.

**Index Ventures — *Rewarding Talent* handbook and OptionPlan / OptionPlan Seed calculators (Dominic Jacquesson):** benchmark grants by role, stage, and geography; senior tech hires average **~1.18%** fully diluted at early stage; a typical 10-person seed team consumes ~**5%** fully diluted (at ~$6M post-money). Index notes US employees get roughly 2x the equity of European counterparts and recommends ~**10% ESOP at seed, 10–15% by Series A**.

## 4. Founder vesting norms

Standard is **4-year monthly vesting, 1-year cliff**, applied to founders' already-issued stock as "reverse vesting" (company repurchase right at cost on unvested shares, lapsing over time; founders file **83(b) elections** within 30 days). Variants: **back-weighted vesting** (e.g., 10/20/30/40 by year, popularized by Amazon for employees; occasionally used for founders to fight early departure). **Acceleration:** *single-trigger* (full vest on change of control) is disfavored — acquirers resist because the team loses retention incentive; *double-trigger* (change of control **plus** termination without cause / good-reason resignation within ~12 months) is the investor-standard, often covering 50–100% of unvested shares. **Re-vesting at fundraising:** Series A investors commonly require founders — especially those with no or expired vesting — to re-subject some or all shares to a new schedule (often with partial credit for time served). **Dead equity** (large stakes held by departed/inactive founders) is a major diligence red flag: investors read >10–15% dead equity as reducing the active team's motivation and their own effective ownership; fixes are negotiated buyouts or retroactive vesting, typically costing months of delay. (Sources: Carta "Vesting Explained"; Capbase "Founder Vesting Schedules"; Startups.com Lexicon; UpCounsel "Reverse Vesting.")

## 5. Frameworks for unequal splits

- **Foundrs.com calculator** (Startup Conference): questionnaire weighting idea origination (a several-point bump), CEO role, who writes the code, business development, commitment level; assumes 4-year vesting and no salaries; treats significant cash separately (as investment, not founder equity).
- **Founder Institute:** "Startup Equity Demystified" advises structured, criteria-based splits and formal founder agreements; its **FAST (Founder/Advisor Standard Template) agreement** benchmarks *advisor* equity: **0.15–0.25%** (standard, ~5 hrs/mo), **0.30–0.50%** (strategic), **0.60–1.0%** (expert), scaled by stage (idea > startup > growth), vesting **monthly over 2 years with a 3-month cliff**; total advisory pools ~5% are common.
- Common weighting factors across frameworks: **idea premium** (~5%, per Shapiro — small, because execution dominates), **CEO premium**, **capital contributed** (best structured as a note/SAFE rather than equity), **opportunity cost** (salary forgone, seniority), **full-time vs. part-time** (part-timers heavily discounted or kept off the founder cap table until full-time).

## 6. Handling inactive founders

Mechanisms: **repurchase rights** on unvested shares at cost (automatic under reverse vesting); negotiated **buybacks of vested shares**; and (more common in UK/EU docs, per **Bird & Bird** and **Ashfords**) **good leaver/bad leaver** provisions — good leavers (death, disability, termination without cause) keep vested shares or sell at **fair market value**; bad leavers (cause, breach, sometimes voluntary resignation) sell at the **lower of cost/nominal value** (e.g., £0.01/share). Unvested shares are forfeited in either case. US practice leans on vesting + right of first refusal rather than aggressive bad-leaver clawbacks; overly punitive vested-share clawbacks are seen as unenforceable/toxic. Slicing Pie's recovery framework (Section 2) is the bootstrapped-stage analog.

## 7. LLC equity: profits interests vs. capital interests

(Sources: Carta "Profits Interest" guide; Lyons Gaddis; The Startup Law Blog.) A **capital interest** shares in current value — if the LLC sold tomorrow, the holder gets proceeds; granting one for services is **taxable compensation** at grant. A **profits interest** shares only in appreciation/profits **above a hurdle** ("threshold value" = FMV at grant, the LLC analog of a stock-option strike price); properly structured under Rev. Proc. 93-27/2001-43 (and IRC §721), grant is **tax-free**, and holders can file a **protective 83(b)**. Mapping corporate concepts: stock options → profits interests with threshold value (but no exercise price to pay, and gains can be capital-gain rather than ordinary income); restricted stock/RSUs → capital interests or unit appreciation rights (phantom units). Caveats: profits-interest holders become **partners** (K-1s, self-employment tax, can't be W-2 employees of the same LLC), and profits interests can **never qualify as QSBS** under §1202 — a key reason venture-track startups incorporate as C-corps instead.
