# Cofounder Ownership and Investor Expectations

**Memo to:** The Founding Team
**From:** Firas
**Re:** Why the cofounders' combined ownership must be secured above 50% before we raise — and why a separate cofounder pool is the right mechanism
**Date:** August 2026

---

## The situation

Under the current plan, the four cofounders are guaranteed only a **20% base — 5% each**. Everything beyond that must be earned from one shared pool, where cofounders and employees compete under identical rules, hour for hour.

Run the numbers honestly: four cofounders against five employees means **employees are the majority of contributors**. At comparable hours, the cofounders' final position computes to roughly:

| Scenario | Cofounders' combined share |
|---|---|
| All four cofounders outwork everyone | ~60%+ |
| Comparable hours across the team | ~56% — the best *typical* case |
| One cofounder falters | ~48% |
| Two falter | ~38% |

So yes — our 20% *can* become more than 50%. **But nothing guarantees it.** The single most investor-visible number on our cap table is currently an accident waiting to be discovered: an output of a work ledger, not a term of the agreement. And we would discover it at the worst possible moment — in diligence, with a term sheet on the table.

## Why this matters to investors

- Across 45,000+ startups, the **median founding team owns 56.2% after the seed round** ([Carta Founder Ownership Report](https://carta.com/data/founder-ownership-2026/)); guidance for *entering* a seed round is founders around [70%](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/founder-ownership-by-round), keeping [at least ~50% through the round](https://www.crv.com/content/startup-equity-structure).
- Dilution compounds. The median team falls to **36% after Series A and 23% after Series B** ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/founder-ownership-2026/)):

| Starting position post-seed | Post-Series A | Post-Series B |
|---|---|---|
| ~56% (median company) | 36% | 23% |
| ~40% (our weak scenarios) | ~26% | ~16% |

  A team that starts the ladder at 40% arrives at Series B where the median company sits **two full rounds later** — thin enough that every future raise begins with the founder-motivation question.
- That question is not superstition. Investors underwrite **7–10 more years of founder commitment**, and the evidence says operators with large personal stakes perform better ([Jensen & Meckling 1976](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0304405X7690026X); [Fahlenbrach 2009](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/empfin/v16y2009i1p136-150.html)). Founders holding less than investors combined is a recognized diligence red flag ([Equity Matrix](https://equitymatrix.io/blog/what-investors-look-for-in-cap-tables)).
- The largest academic study of founder equity ([Hellmann & Wasserman, *Management Science* 2016](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2474); 3,700+ founders) delivers the two facts that frame this decision: badly structured splits cost real money — a valuation penalty on the order of **10% of company value at first financing** — and **renegotiating a split after signing rarely succeeds**, because whoever the current structure favors holds a veto. Whatever we believe the right number is, the only time we can set it is *before* we sign.

## Why cofounders have their own pool

Because an hour is not just an hour — *when* it was worked and *what was staked on it* matter, and a shared pool prices neither:

1. **Founding risk is priced everywhere else — except in our pool.** The cofounders committed when the company was nothing: no product, no team, no validation, no salary in sight. Employees joined a working team with momentum. Capital markets price this stage difference explicitly — a dollar invested at founding buys many times the equity of a dollar at Series A. A shared hours-based pool is the only place in the startup economy where the earliest, riskiest commitment earns the *same* rate as the safest. The separate pool corrects that mispricing.
2. **Founders carry liabilities employees never touch.** Managing founders bear personal legal exposure, sign whatever must be signed, own payroll and survival, and cannot resign without the company collapsing. An employee's downside is bounded at a lost job; a founder's is not. Equity is the only compensation for unlimited, uninsurable downside.
3. **Investors underwrite the founders specifically.** The market's revealed structure — median founders at 56%, median employee pool at 12.1% ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/founder-ownership-2026/)) — is not tradition; it is thousands of investors pricing who must still be there, fully committed, in year eight. A cap table that doesn't distinguish founders from employees tells investors we don't either.
4. **Fairness is preserved where fairness belongs.** Within each pool, contribution still decides everything: a cofounder who slacks loses to cofounders who deliver; an employee who excels out-earns every other employee. The class split prices risk, liability, and permanence — things hours cannot measure. The pools price effort — the thing hours measure well. Each mechanism does the job it is actually suited for.

## The proposal

| Layer | Size | How it's earned |
|---|---|---|
| Cofounder base (4 × 5%) | 20% | Vests over time while active |
| Cofounder contribution pool | 35–40% | Earned by contribution, competed among cofounders only |
| **Cofounders combined** | **55–60% — ensured by structure** | |

The remaining ~40–45% serves employees and future hires under the same contribution rules as today. Nothing about the plan's philosophy changes: every share in every layer is still earned, nothing is granted, and inactivity still costs you. The only change is that the split between classes becomes a **decision we make deliberately** — landing the cofounders exactly where funded companies stand when they successfully raise.

## The ask

Agree the cofounder pool (35–40%) before we ratify the equity plan. The logic is asymmetric and that is the point: if the ledger would have carried us above 50% anyway, this costs nothing and changes nothing. If it wouldn't, this is the difference between walking into our seed round looking like the companies that get funded — and spending every investor meeting explaining why we don't. There is no scenario where deciding our own number, now, on the evidence, leaves the company worse off.
